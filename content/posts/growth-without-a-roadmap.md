There's a version of this story where I had a plan.

Where I graduated, landed a role at a startup, climbed the ladder with intention. That version doesn't exist. What exists instead is this: a one-way ticket to Bangalore, ₹12,000 in my account, and a blind conviction that *something* would work out.

---

## The Night Shift as a Classroom

I worked bar shifts to fund my studies. That sentence sounds dramatic — it was, in the way that most honest things are. But what I actually learned behind that counter was more useful than most curricula:

- **People are the product.** Whether you're selling a cocktail or an enterprise plan, the thing you're really selling is *confidence* in you.
- **Speed of feedback loops matters.** In a bar, you know in 20 seconds if a drink is wrong. Most companies wait 6 months for a post-mortem.
- **Exhaustion is a filter.** The things you keep doing when you're tired are the things you actually care about.

---

## GTM Is Just the Beginning

When I made the jump to Growth full-time, I thought GTM strategy was the job. It's not — it's the *starting gun*. The real work is what happens after:

1. **The pipeline that nobody owns** — someone has to care about leads from day 60 to day 120.
2. **The product loop nobody maps** — activation → engagement → referral is a living system, not a slide.
3. **The human who almost churned** — and why they didn't.

Growth is fundamentally about *reducing distance*: the distance between a problem and its solution, between a user and "aha", between a founder and their first 100 customers.

---

## What Samurai Champloo Taught Me About Strategy

I'm serious about this. Mugen's style — *Champloo*, a chaotic blend of breakdance and swordfighting — is the best metaphor for scrappy GTM I've found.

> *"Don't live your life making excuses. Just run."*

Pure chaos isn't sustainable. But **disciplined improvisation** — knowing your fundamentals well enough to break them — is a genuine edge. Most growth teams are either too rigid (playbook-only) or too chaotic (no playbook). The craft is living in between.
